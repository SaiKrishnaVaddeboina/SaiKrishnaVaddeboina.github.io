The Problem

Mental health crises in higher ed are usually caught too late.

By the time a student reaches the campus counseling center, the academic and personal damage is often already done. The signals were there earlier — sleep patterns, GPA decline, academic pressure, family stressors — but no one was watching the right combination.

This project asks: can a model surface elevated depression risk early enough to prompt a voluntary outreach — not as surveillance, but as a flag for human counselors to follow up?

The Approach

Three models, one shared feature pipeline.

01

Logistic Regression

Baseline model. Interpretable coefficients map directly onto risk factors — useful for explaining "why" to a domain expert who isn't a data scientist.

02

Random Forest

Ensemble of decision trees handles non-linear feature interactions natively. Provides feature importance rankings that informed the final feature engineering pass.

03

XGBoost

Gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Best performer on AUC and recall. Tuned via grid search over max_depth, learning_rate, and n_estimators.

04

Stratified K-Fold CV

5-fold cross-validation stratified on the target class to handle the natural class imbalance (depressed vs. non-depressed students). Ensures every fold sees both classes.

Feature Engineering

The signal is in the interactions.

Categorical encoding

One-hot encoding for nominal features (degree program, dietary habits); ordinal encoding for naturally ranked features (sleep quality, academic pressure on a 1–5 scale).

Binning continuous variables

Age and GPA binned into intervals to capture non-linear effects — a 0.1 GPA difference matters very differently at 3.9 vs. 2.1.

Interaction variables

Hand-crafted interactions like academic_pressure × sleep_hours and financial_stress × family_history — capturing compound risk that no single feature surfaces alone.

Class imbalance handling

Used scale_pos_weight in XGBoost and class_weight='balanced' in scikit-learn models to prevent the majority class from dominating the loss.

Results

XGBoost wins on recall — and recall is what matters here.

For a depression-screening model, false negatives are the costly error. Missing a student who needs help is worse than over-flagging a few who don't. So we optimized for recall rather than raw accuracy, with AUC as the secondary tiebreaker.

The Ethics Layer

A high-recall model is not automatically a deployable system.

The deliverable wasn't just the model — it was a set of ethical guardrails for how a system like this should and shouldn't be used in higher education:

  • Voluntary participation — students should opt in, with full disclosure of what's being modeled and why.
  • Output goes to humans, not actions — the model surfaces flags for trained counselors to evaluate, never triggers automated interventions.
  • No academic consequences — flags must be firewalled from grading, financial aid, and disciplinary processes.
  • Right to explanation — students flagged should be able to ask "why" and get a meaningful answer (this is where the Logistic Regression baseline keeps its value).
  • Continuous bias auditing — re-evaluate model performance across demographic subgroups every term to catch drift and disparate impact.
What I Learned

The hardest part of an ML project is rarely the model.

The XGBoost training took an afternoon. The recommendations — which guardrails matter, who gets to see the output, what the failure modes look like in practice — took the rest of the project. That's the real work in applied ML, and it's the part you can't automate.
